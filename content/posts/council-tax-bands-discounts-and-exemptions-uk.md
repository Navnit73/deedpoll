# Council Tax Bands, Discounts and Exemptions: A UK Guide

Council Tax is one of the biggest household bills most people pay, and it is also one of the easiest to overpay. Discounts are often not applied automatically, exemptions are poorly understood, and many people never check whether their home is in the right band at all.

This guide explains how Council Tax works in England, Wales and Scotland, why Northern Ireland is different, who is responsible for paying, and the discounts, exemptions and reductions you may be able to claim. It also covers how to challenge your band safely and what to do when you move. It is general information, not personal financial advice — your council's website will have the details and rates that apply to you.

---

## How Council Tax Works

Council Tax is a tax on domestic property, collected by your local council to help pay for services like bin collections, social care, libraries and local roads. Each home is placed in a **valuation band** based on what it would have been worth at a fixed historic date. The council sets a charge for a Band D property each year, and every other band pays a fixed proportion of that Band D figure.

Two things follow from this:

- **Your band is not based on today's value.** A house worth £400,000 today might still be in Band D because of what it was worth decades ago.
- **The amount you pay depends on where you live.** Two homes in the same band in different council areas can have very different bills, because each council (plus any police, fire and parish authorities) sets its own Band D charge.

### The Four Nations at a Glance

| Nation | System | Valuation date | Number of bands | Who assesses bands |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| England | Council Tax | 1 April 1991 | 8 (A–H) | Valuation Office Agency |
| Scotland | Council Tax | 1 April 1991 | 8 (A–H) | Scottish Assessors |
| Wales | Council Tax | 1 April 2003 | 9 (A–I) | Valuation Office Agency |
| Northern Ireland | Domestic rates | Capital value basis | No bands | Land & Property Services |

## Council Tax Bands in England

English bands are based on 1 April 1991 values. The ratio shows how much each band pays compared with Band D.

| Band | 1991 value | Proportion of Band D |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| A | Up to £40,000 | 6/9 |
| B | £40,001 – £52,000 | 7/9 |
| C | £52,001 – £68,000 | 8/9 |
| D | £68,001 – £88,000 | 9/9 |
| E | £88,001 – £120,000 | 11/9 |
| F | £120,001 – £160,000 | 13/9 |
| G | £160,001 – £320,000 | 15/9 |
| H | Over £320,000 | 18/9 |

**Worked example:** If your council's Band D charge is £2,250 for the year, a Band B home pays 7/9 of that: £2,250 × 7 ÷ 9 = £1,750. A Band H home pays double Band D: £4,500. This is an illustrative figure only — check your council's published charges.

## Council Tax Bands in Scotland

Scotland also uses 1991 values, but with lower thresholds:

| Band | 1991 value |
| :--- | :--- |
| A | Up to £27,000 |
| B | £27,001 – £35,000 |
| C | £35,001 – £45,000 |
| D | £45,001 – £58,000 |
| E | £58,001 – £80,000 |
| F | £80,001 – £106,000 |
| G | £106,001 – £212,000 |
| H | Over £212,000 |

The proportions for the higher bands in Scotland were changed in 2017, so they differ from England. Your local council or the Scottish Government website lists the current ratios.

## Council Tax Bands in Wales

Wales was revalued using 1 April 2003 values and has an extra top band, Band I:

| Band | 2003 value |
| :--- | :--- |
| A | Up to £44,000 |
| B | £44,001 – £65,000 |
| C | £65,001 – £91,000 |
| D | £91,001 – £123,000 |
| E | £123,001 – £162,000 |
| F | £162,001 – £223,000 |
| G | £223,001 – £324,000 |
| H | £324,001 – £424,000 |
| I | Over £424,000 |

The Welsh Government has been working on further Council Tax reform, including a revaluation, so check its website for the latest position.

## Northern Ireland: Domestic Rates

Northern Ireland does not have Council Tax. Instead, households pay **domestic rates**, calculated by multiplying the capital value of the property by a regional rate and a district rate. Bills are issued by Land & Property Services. There are separate schemes such as rate relief and a lone pensioner allowance, so check nidirect for what applies to you.

## Who Has to Pay

Council Tax is normally paid by the adult resident with the strongest legal interest in the property. In practice:

- **Owner-occupiers** pay for their own home.
- **Tenants** usually pay, unless they live in a house in multiple occupation (HMO) where the landlord is liable, or the tenancy agreement says rent includes Council Tax.
- **Joint occupiers** — such as couples or joint tenants — are "jointly and severally liable", meaning the council can chase any of them for the whole bill.
- **Empty properties** are usually the owner's responsibility.

Under-18s never pay Council Tax.

## Discounts

### The 25% Single Person Discount

If you are the only adult living in your home, you can claim a 25% discount. This is the single most valuable and most commonly missed reduction. It is not just for people who live entirely alone: you still qualify if every other adult in your household is "disregarded" (see below).

On the illustrative £1,750 Band B bill above, the single person discount saves £437.50 a year, reducing the bill to £1,312.50.

Councils run regular reviews of single person discounts. If someone moves in, you must tell the council, or you could face a backdated bill and a possible penalty.

### People Who Are "Disregarded"

When the council counts the adults in a property, certain people are ignored. These include:

- full-time students, student nurses and apprentices
- people who are severely mentally impaired (with a supporting medical certificate and qualifying benefit)
- some carers, typically those caring for someone who is not their partner or child under 18, for a set number of hours per week
- people under 18, and 18- and 19-year-olds still in full-time education or just left school or college
- diplomats and some members of visiting forces
- people in hospital, care homes or prison (in most cases)

If one adult remains after the disregards, you get a 25% discount. If everyone in the property is disregarded, you may get a larger discount, or the property may be fully exempt.

### Disabled Band Reduction Scheme

If your home has been adapted or has features that are essential for a disabled person living there — for example a second bathroom or kitchen, an extra room used mainly for their needs, or enough space to use a wheelchair indoors — your bill can be charged as if the property were one band lower. If you are already in the lowest band, councils in England can still apply a reduction. Apply to your council; you do not need to be the bill payer yourself to qualify, as the disabled person can be anyone who lives there.

## Exemptions

Some properties do not pay Council Tax at all. Common exemptions include:

- **Homes occupied only by full-time students** (including student halls)
- homes where everyone living there is severely mentally impaired
- homes occupied only by people under 18
- empty properties left by someone who has moved into a care home or hospital, or to provide or receive care
- empty properties where the liable person has died, for a period while probate is sorted out
- annexes occupied by a dependent relative

Exemption rules vary in detail between England, Wales and Scotland, and the council decides each case, so always check its website and apply in writing.

## Council Tax Reduction (Council Tax Support)

If you are on a low income or claim benefits such as Universal Credit, you may be entitled to **Council Tax Reduction** (also called Council Tax Support). In England, each council designs its own working-age scheme, so the amount varies widely — some cover most of the bill, others only part. Pensioners are covered by a national scheme. Scotland and Wales have their own national schemes.

Claiming Universal Credit does **not** automatically apply Council Tax Reduction. You must make a separate application to your council, and you should do so promptly as backdating is limited.

## Second Homes and Empty Homes

Councils can charge more, not less, on homes that nobody lives in as their main residence:

- **Empty homes premium (England):** Councils can charge a premium on homes that have been empty and substantially unfurnished for a qualifying period, with higher premiums possible for properties left empty for many years.
- **Second homes premium (England):** From April 2025, councils in England can charge up to a 100% premium on furnished homes that are no one's main residence — effectively doubling the bill.
- **Wales and Scotland:** Councils also have powers to charge premiums on second and long-term empty homes, and these can be higher than in England.

There are exceptions, for example for homes being actively marketed for sale or let, or for job-related accommodation, so check your council's policy before assuming a premium applies.

## How to Check and Challenge Your Band

### Step 1: Compare with Your Neighbours

Use the VOA's Check your Council Tax band service (England and Wales) or the Scottish Assessors website. Look at similar homes on your street — same type, size and age. If your home is in a higher band than nearly identical neighbours, it may be worth a closer look.

### Step 2: Gather Evidence

Useful evidence includes the bands of comparable homes and, where you can find it, what similar properties sold for around 1991 (or 2003 in Wales). Online estimators that work backwards from today's prices are only a rough guide.

### Step 3: Ask for a Review

Request a review through GOV.UK (England and Wales) or your local Assessor (Scotland). There are specific grounds and time limits for formal appeals — for instance, after you become the taxpayer at a new address — so check the current rules before applying.

### The Risk: Your Band Can Go Up

A review is not one-way. The assessor can decide your home is in the **wrong band in the other direction** and move it up. If that happens, neighbouring properties of the same type may also be looked at. Only request a review if you have solid evidence that your band is too high.

## Moving Home

When you move, Council Tax is charged daily, so you only pay for the days you are liable at each address.

1. Tell your old council the date you are leaving and give a forwarding address. You will receive a final bill or a refund.
2. Register with your new council from the day you move in. Many councils let you do this online.
3. Reapply for any discounts — single person discount, disregards, Council Tax Reduction — because they do not transfer automatically.
4. Check the band of your new home before you buy or rent, and factor it into your budget. Our [mortgage affordability calculator](/uk-mortgage-affordability-calculator) and [stamp duty calculator](/calculate-stamp-duty-england) can help you see the bigger picture of moving costs.

If you have also changed your name, your council is one of many organisations to update — see [who to notify after changing your name](/name-change/who-to-notify-after-changing-your-name-uk).

## Paying in 10 or 12 Instalments

By default, councils usually spread the annual bill over 10 monthly payments from April to January, leaving February and March free. You have the right to ask for **12 instalments** instead, which lowers each payment.

Using the illustrative £1,750 bill: 10 instalments are £175 a month; 12 instalments are about £145.83 a month. Paying by Direct Debit is usually simplest and avoids missed payments. If you miss an instalment, the council will send a reminder; repeated missed payments can mean you lose the right to pay in instalments and the whole year becomes due, so contact the council early if you are struggling.

## Summary

Your Council Tax bill depends on your property's band — based on 1991 values in England and Scotland and 2003 values in Wales — and on what your local council charges for Band D. Northern Ireland uses domestic rates instead. The biggest savings come from claiming what you are entitled to: the 25% single person discount, disregards for students, apprentices and carers, exemptions for all-student homes, Council Tax Reduction on a low income, and the Disabled Band Reduction scheme. Check your band against similar homes, but remember a review can move it up as well as down. When you move, tell both councils and reapply for discounts, and ask for 12 instalments if that makes budgeting easier.
