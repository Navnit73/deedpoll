# Enrolled vs Unenrolled Deed Poll: Which Do You Need?

If you're researching how to change your name in the UK, you'll quickly come across two kinds of deed poll: enrolled and unenrolled. Some websites suggest that enrolment is the "official" or "proper" option, and charge accordingly. The reality is simpler. For almost everyone, an unenrolled deed poll does the job perfectly well, costs nothing to make yourself, and keeps your name change private.

This guide explains what each type is, how enrolment works, why most people don't need it, and the practical details of signing and witnessing your deed poll correctly.

## What Is an Unenrolled Deed Poll?

A deed poll is a legal document in which you declare that you're abandoning your old name, will use your new name for all purposes, and require everyone to address you by it. "Poll" refers to the fact that it's a deed made by one person only.

An unenrolled deed poll is simply a deed poll that you sign and have witnessed, then keep yourself. It isn't registered anywhere. It's legally binding on you as soon as it's properly signed and witnessed, because it's executed as a deed.

There's no official template you must use, but organisations expect certain contents:

1. Your old name and your new name, in full
2. A declaration that you're abandoning your old name
3. A declaration that you'll use your new name at all times
4. A requirement for all persons to address you by your new name
5. The date
6. Your signature in both your old and new names
7. Your witness's signature, full name and address

## What Is an Enrolled Deed Poll?

An enrolled deed poll is one that has been formally recorded, or "enrolled", in the Enrolment Books of the Senior Courts. In England and Wales this is done through the Royal Courts of Justice in London, within the King's Bench Division. Once enrolled, the change of name is publicised in The Gazette, the UK's official public record.

Enrolment doesn't give your deed poll any extra legal force. What it provides is an official, permanent record held by the court, from which official copies can be obtained later.

### The Enrolment Process in Outline

The details are set out in court rules and the current forms on GOV.UK, but broadly:

1. **Check you're eligible.** Enrolment is generally only available to Commonwealth citizens, which includes British citizens. If you're not a Commonwealth citizen, you can't usually enrol, although you can still use an unenrolled deed poll.
2. **Prepare the deed poll in the prescribed form.** An enrolled deed poll must follow a specific wording set by regulations, and it's prepared on the court's forms.
3. **Provide supporting evidence.** You'll typically need proof of your citizenship and a declaration from someone who has known you for a number of years, confirming your identity.
4. **Submit and pay the fee.** Send your application to the Royal Courts of Justice with the fee. Check the current fee on GOV.UK.
5. **Publication.** Your change of name is published in The Gazette.
6. **Receive your enrolled deed poll.** The court returns the enrolled document, and official copies can be ordered later.

Applications for children are possible but carry extra requirements, including the consent of everyone with parental responsibility.

## The Privacy Issue

This is the biggest reason most people choose not to enrol. Publication in The Gazette means your old name and new name become a permanent public record that anyone can search online. For many people that doesn't matter, but for others it's a serious concern:

- People escaping domestic abuse or harassment
- Trans and non-binary people who don't want their previous name linked publicly to their new one
- People changing their name to distance themselves from family or a past event
- Anyone in the public eye

Once a notice has been published, you shouldn't assume it can be removed. If privacy matters to you, an unenrolled deed poll keeps your name change between you and the organisations you choose to tell.

## Who Actually Needs an Enrolled Deed Poll?

Very few people. HM Passport Office, the DVLA, HMRC, the DWP, the NHS, banks and building societies all routinely accept unenrolled deed polls. Enrolment may occasionally be requested by a specific organisation or overseas authority, or chosen by someone who wants a court-held record for their own reasons. If an organisation does tell you that it needs enrolment, ask it to confirm in writing; it's sometimes a misunderstanding of what a deed poll is.

If an organisation rejects your unenrolled deed poll, it's usually because of a problem with how it was drawn up or witnessed rather than because it wasn't enrolled. Our guide on [what to do if your deed poll was rejected](/my-deed-poll-was-rejected) covers the common reasons and fixes.

## When Enrolment Isn't Available or Advisable

- **You're not a Commonwealth citizen.** Enrolment generally isn't open to you, but an unenrolled deed poll still works.
- **You live in Scotland or were born there.** Scotland has no enrolment system. People born or adopted in Scotland can apply to National Records of Scotland to have a change of name recorded, and deed polls are less commonly used there.
- **Northern Ireland.** Northern Ireland has its own court arrangements. Check NI Direct for current guidance before applying.
- **You need your new name quickly.** Enrolment takes weeks; an unenrolled deed poll is ready the moment it's witnessed.
- **Privacy matters to you.** See above.

## Witness Requirements

A deed must be signed in the presence of a witness who then signs to confirm they saw you sign. To make sure your deed poll is accepted, follow these rules:

1. **Use one independent adult witness.** A friend, neighbour or colleague aged 18 or over is fine.
2. **Don't use a relative, your partner or someone you live with.** Organisations such as HM Passport Office expect an independent witness, and using a family member is a common reason for rejection.
3. **Sign in front of them.** The witness must actually see you sign, not sign later.
4. **Sign in both names.** Sign once in your old name and once in your new name.
5. **The witness adds their details.** Their signature, full name and address.
6. **Don't use correction fluid.** If you make a mistake, start a fresh copy.

You don't need a solicitor to witness an unenrolled deed poll, and there's no fee for having one witnessed by a friend.

---

## How Many Copies to Make

Sign several originals at the same session, with your witness signing each one. Every copy signed this way is an original. Many people make between five and ten, because:

- HM Passport Office and the DVLA usually want an original and return it later
- Some banks want to see an original in branch
- Posting originals to several organisations at once speeds things up

Keep them flat and safe, and keep a note of which organisation has which copy. Our [name change checklist](/checklist) helps with this.

This guide is general information, not legal advice. If your situation is complex, for example a dispute about a child's name, get advice from a solicitor or Citizens Advice.

---

## Summary

Both enrolled and unenrolled deed polls are legally valid. An unenrolled deed poll is free to make, private, ready immediately and accepted by UK government departments and banks, which is why it's the right choice for almost everyone. Enrolment through the Royal Courts of Justice creates a court record and a permanent public notice in The Gazette, takes weeks, costs a fee and is generally limited to Commonwealth citizens. Whichever you choose, sign several originals in front of one independent adult witness who isn't a relative, and you'll have what you need to update your records.
