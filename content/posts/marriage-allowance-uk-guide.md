# Marriage Allowance: A Complete UK Guide

Marriage Allowance is one of the simplest tax breaks in the UK system, yet many eligible couples have never claimed it. If one of you earns little or nothing and the other is a basic-rate taxpayer, you could reduce your household tax bill by up to £252 a year — and claim back up to four previous years on top.

This guide covers who qualifies, exactly how the saving is worked out, how to apply, what happens when your circumstances change, and the mistakes that trip people up. Figures relate to the 2026/27 tax year (6 April 2026 to 5 April 2027).

---

## What Is Marriage Allowance?

Everyone in the UK has a tax-free Personal Allowance, which is £12,570 for 2026/27. If you earn less than that, part of your allowance goes unused. Marriage Allowance lets you transfer **£1,260** of your allowance to your husband, wife or civil partner, so it can reduce the tax they pay instead.

The lower earner's allowance falls to £11,310, and the recipient effectively gets £13,830 of tax-free income. Because the extra £1,260 would otherwise have been taxed at 20%, the recipient saves up to **£252** a year.

---

## Who Is Eligible?

You can claim if **all** of the following apply:

- You're married or in a civil partnership (living together isn't enough)
- The lower earner (the transferor) has income below the Personal Allowance, normally under £12,570
- The higher earner (the recipient) pays Income Tax at the basic rate, which in England, Wales and Northern Ireland means income between £12,571 and £50,270
- Neither of you was born before 6 April 1935 (if one of you was, see Married Couple's Allowance below)

Marriage Allowance **isn't available** if either partner is a higher-rate or additional-rate taxpayer.

### What Counts as Income?

Income isn't just wages. It includes pensions, self-employed profit, rental profit and savings interest. Someone with a small salary and a large amount of savings interest might have income above the Personal Allowance even if their payslip suggests otherwise, so add everything up before applying.

| Situation | Can you claim? |

| Married, one earns £9,000, the other £60,000 (higher rate) | No |
| Married, both earn over £12,570 | No |
| Married, one partner born before 6 April 1935 | Consider Married Couple's Allowance instead |

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: The Full £252 Saving

Sam earns £8,000 a year from part-time work. Alex earns £32,000 and lives in England.

- Sam transfers £1,260. Sam's allowance falls to £11,310. Since Sam earns £8,000, Sam still pays no Income Tax.
- Alex's tax-free income rises from £12,570 to £13,830.
- Alex's taxable income falls by £1,260. At 20%, that's **£252 less tax** a year.

The household is £252 better off, with no downside.

### Example 2: A Partial Saving

Jordan earns £12,000. Priya earns £40,000.

- Jordan transfers £1,260, so Jordan's allowance falls to £11,310.
- Jordan now pays tax on £12,000 − £11,310 = £690. At 20%, that's £138.
- Priya saves £252.
- **Net household saving: £252 − £138 = £114 a year.**

Still worth doing, but much less than the headline figure. The closer the lower earner's income gets to £12,570, the smaller the net gain.

### Example 3: Backdating

If you've been eligible since at least April 2022 and claim during 2026/27, you can backdate to 2022/23, 2023/24, 2024/25 and 2025/26. The Personal Allowance has been £12,570 in each of those years, so the transferable amount has been £1,260, worth up to £252 a year.

- 4 backdated years × £252 = up to **£1,008**, paid as a lump sum
- Plus up to £252 for the current year through the recipient's tax code
- **Total: up to £1,260**

You can check how the change affects your monthly take-home pay with our [after-tax pay calculator](/after-tax-pay-calculator-uk).

## How to Apply

### Step 1: Gather Your Details

You'll need both of your National Insurance numbers and some identity information for the lower earner, such as a passport number or a recent payslip or P60.

### Step 2: Apply on GOV.UK

The **lower earner** makes the claim, because they're the one giving away part of their allowance. Search "Marriage Allowance" on GOV.UK and follow the link to apply online. You'll be asked to sign in with your Government Gateway details or create an account. The process is free.

### Step 3: Choose the Years

During the application, you'll be asked whether you want to claim for the current tax year, and whether you want to backdate. Select each previous year in which you were both eligible.

### Step 4: Watch for Tax Code Changes

Once approved:

- The recipient's tax code changes to end in **M** (for example, 1383M)
- The transferor's code changes to end in **N** (for example, 1131N), if they have a tax code at all

Our guide on [how to check your Income Tax](/tax-money/how-to-check-your-income-tax-uk) explains where to see your tax code and how it was worked out.

If you can't apply online, you can call the Income Tax helpline on 0300 200 3300.

---

## Backdated Claims and How They're Paid

The current year's benefit is normally given through the recipient's tax code, so they see slightly more take-home pay each month. Backdated years work differently: HMRC recalculates each earlier year and pays the difference as a **lump sum**, usually by bank transfer or cheque.

If the recipient files a Self Assessment return, the allowance is dealt with through the return instead.

The four-year limit is strict. Each year that passes, the oldest year drops out of reach, so if you think you qualify, claim sooner rather than later.

## When Your Circumstances Change

Marriage Allowance renews automatically, so it's your responsibility to tell HMRC when something changes.

### If the Lower Earner's Income Rises

If the transferor's income goes above the Personal Allowance, the claim may no longer make sense — and if the recipient becomes a higher-rate taxpayer, you're no longer eligible. The transferor should cancel. If you cancel, the allowance normally continues until the end of the current tax year.

### Divorce or Dissolution

If your marriage or civil partnership ends, the transferor should contact HMRC to cancel. The allowance may run to the end of the tax year, or in some cases be backdated to the start of that tax year, which could leave the recipient with a small amount of tax to pay. Check GOV.UK or contact HMRC for how this applies to you.

### If a Partner Dies

If the partner who received the allowance dies, their estate is treated as having the higher allowance for that year, and the surviving partner's own allowance returns to normal. If the partner who transferred the allowance dies, the recipient generally keeps the higher allowance until the end of that tax year. A surviving partner can still make a backdated claim for years when both were alive and eligible — contact HMRC to arrange this.

### Changing Your Name After Marriage

If you've recently married and changed your surname, make sure HMRC has your new name before applying, so that both records match. Our guide on [how to change your name after marriage](/how-to-change-name-after-marriage-uk) explains who to notify.

## Marriage Allowance in Scotland

Marriage Allowance is available across the UK, including Scotland. However, Scotland has its own Income Tax bands — starter, basic, intermediate, higher, advanced and top — so the eligibility test for the recipient is based on the Scottish bands rather than the £50,270 threshold used elsewhere. In broad terms, the recipient must not be paying tax at the Scottish higher rate or above. Because of the different rates, the exact saving for a Scottish taxpayer may differ slightly from £252. Check GOV.UK for the current Scottish thresholds before applying.

## Marriage Allowance vs Married Couple's Allowance

| | Marriage Allowance | Married Couple's Allowance |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Who it's for | Married couples and civil partners | Couples where at least one partner was born before 6 April 1935 |
| How it works | Transfers £1,260 of Personal Allowance | Reduces the tax bill directly by 10% of the allowance amount |
| Income test | Recipient must be a basic-rate taxpayer | No higher-rate bar, though the value reduces at higher incomes |
| Typical value | Up to £252 a year | Usually more — check GOV.UK for current figures |

You can't claim both. If either of you was born before 6 April 1935, look at Married Couple's Allowance first.

## Common Mistakes

- **The wrong partner applies.** The lower earner must make the claim.
- **Forgetting non-wage income.** Pensions, savings interest and rental profit all count.
- **Claiming when the recipient is a higher-rate taxpayer.** Even if only just over the threshold, you're not eligible, and HMRC may take the money back.
- **Not backdating.** Many couples claim for the current year only and miss up to £1,008.
- **Failing to cancel.** If incomes change, the claim continues automatically, which may lead to an underpayment.
- **Assuming living together counts.** You must be legally married or in a civil partnership.

---

## Beware Refund-Claim Companies

Some firms advertise that they'll "find" your Marriage Allowance refund. In reality, they're submitting the same free GOV.UK application you could do yourself in a few minutes, and many take a significant percentage of the refund as a fee. Some ask you to sign forms that redirect your refund to them. Claim directly through GOV.UK and keep the whole amount.

If your situation is complex — for example, irregular income or self-employment — an accountant or HMRC can help confirm eligibility.

---

## Summary

Marriage Allowance lets a lower-earning spouse or civil partner transfer £1,260 of their Personal Allowance, saving the basic-rate partner up to £252 a year. You can backdate four years, paid as a lump sum, for a total of up to £1,260. The lower earner applies for free on GOV.UK, the claim renews automatically, and you must cancel it if incomes change or the relationship ends. Scottish taxpayers can claim too, but check the Scottish bands. And if either of you was born before 6 April 1935, Married Couple's Allowance is likely to be the better option.
