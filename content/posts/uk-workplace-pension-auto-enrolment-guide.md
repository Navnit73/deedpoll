# Workplace Pensions and Auto-Enrolment: A Complete UK Guide

Since auto-enrolment was introduced, millions of people in the UK have started saving into a workplace pension without having to make a decision at all. That is a good thing, but it also means many people have pensions they have never looked at, contributions they do not understand, and old pots they have lost track of.

This guide explains how auto-enrolment works for the 2026/27 tax year, who qualifies, what the minimum contributions are, how tax relief is added, and what to do when you change jobs, change your name, or want to find an old pension. It also covers the basics of the State Pension. It is general information, not personal financial advice. For guidance on your own circumstances, the government-backed MoneyHelper service offers free, impartial help, and a regulated financial adviser can give personalised advice.

---

## What Auto-Enrolment Is

Under the Pensions Act 2008, every employer in the UK must put eligible staff into a qualifying workplace pension scheme and pay into it. You do not need to sign up — your employer must enrol you automatically and tell you in writing when they have done so, including how much will be paid in and how to opt out.

Most auto-enrolment schemes are **defined contribution** pensions: the money paid in by you, your employer and the government is invested, and the size of your pot at retirement depends on how much went in and how the investments performed. Some employers, particularly in the public sector, offer **defined benefit** (final salary or career average) schemes instead, which promise a set income.

---

## Who Is Eligible

Your status depends on your age and your earnings from that particular job.

| Your situation | Status | Automatically enrolled? | Employer must contribute? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Aged 22 to State Pension age, earning over £10,000 a year | Eligible jobholder | Yes | Yes |
| Aged 16–21 or State Pension age to 74, earning over £10,000 | Non-eligible jobholder | No, but you can opt in | Yes, if you opt in |
| Aged 16–74, earning between about £6,240 and £10,000 | Non-eligible jobholder | No, but you can opt in | Yes, if you opt in |
| Aged 16–74, earning about £6,240 or less | Entitled worker | No, but you can ask to join | Not required |

The £10,000 trigger applies per job. If you have two part-time jobs each paying £7,000, neither employer has to auto-enrol you, though you can ask both to let you join. Thresholds are reviewed each year by the government, so check GOV.UK if your pay is close to a boundary.

## Minimum Contributions

The legal minimum total contribution is **8% of qualifying earnings**. Your employer must pay at least **3%**, and the rest normally comes from you, with part of your share coming from tax relief.

| Who pays | Minimum % of qualifying earnings |
| :--- | :--- |
| Employer | 3% |
| You (including tax relief) | 5% |
| **Total** | **8%** |

Many employers pay more than the minimum, and some will match higher contributions from you — for example, paying 6% if you pay 6%. If your employer offers matching, it is often one of the most valuable benefits in your package.

### What Are "Qualifying Earnings"?

Minimum contributions are usually based on **qualifying earnings** — your gross pay (including overtime, bonuses, commission and statutory pay) between a lower limit of about £6,240 and an upper limit of £50,270 a year. The first £6,240 or so is ignored. Some employers calculate contributions on your full basic salary instead, which is often more generous; your scheme documents will say which applies.

### Worked Example: £30,000 Salary

- Qualifying earnings: £30,000 − £6,240 = **£23,760** a year
- Total minimum contribution at 8%: £23,760 × 0.08 = **£1,900.80** a year
- Employer's 3%: £23,760 × 0.03 = **£712.80** a year (£59.40 a month)
- Your 5%: £23,760 × 0.05 = **£1,188** a year (£99 a month gross)

Because of tax relief, your 5% does not all come out of your take-home pay. For a basic rate taxpayer, the £1,188 costs you £950.40 (£79.20 a month), with the remaining £237.60 coming from basic rate tax relief. So for a real cost of £950.40, £1,900.80 goes into your pension each year. You can see how pension contributions affect your take-home pay with our [after tax pay calculator](/after-tax-pay-calculator-uk).

## How Tax Relief Works

Pension contributions get tax relief at your highest rate of income tax, within annual limits. Workplace schemes add it in one of two ways, and the difference matters more than most people realise.

### Net Pay Arrangement

Your contribution is taken from your salary **before** income tax is calculated. You pay tax on a lower amount, so you get relief at your highest rate automatically. In the example above, £99 a month is deducted, but your tax falls by £19.80, so your take-home pay drops by £79.20.

### Relief at Source

Your contribution is taken **after** tax. You pay 80% of the gross amount — £79.20 in our example — and the pension provider claims the other 20% (£19.80) from HMRC and adds it to your pot. If you are a higher or additional rate taxpayer, you can claim the extra relief through your Self Assessment tax return or by contacting HMRC.

### The Low-Earner Problem with Net Pay

If you earn less than the £12,570 Personal Allowance, you pay no income tax, so under a net pay arrangement there is no tax to reduce — you get **no relief** through payroll. Under relief at source, you still get the 20% top-up even though you pay no tax.

**Example:** You earn £11,000 a year and opt in. Qualifying earnings are £11,000 − £6,240 = £4,760, so your 5% share is £238. Under relief at source, you pay £190.40 and the government adds £47.60. Under net pay, you pay the full £238 yourself. The government has introduced a system to make top-up payments to some low earners in net pay schemes, so check GOV.UK to see whether you qualify and how it is paid.

## Salary Sacrifice

Some employers offer pension contributions through **salary sacrifice** (sometimes called salary exchange). You agree to a lower contractual salary, and your employer pays the difference into your pension as an employer contribution. Because your salary is lower, you pay less income tax **and** less National Insurance.

**Example:** On £30,000, sacrificing the same £1,188 a year:

- Your salary becomes £28,812
- Income tax saving: £1,188 × 20% = £237.60
- NI saving: £1,188 × 8% = £95.04
- Your take-home pay falls by £855.36 rather than £950.40

Many employers also pass on some or all of their own NI saving. Points to check before agreeing:

- A lower salary can affect things linked to it, such as mortgage applications, life cover, and some statutory payments.
- Salary sacrifice cannot take your pay below the National Minimum Wage.
- The government has announced plans to limit National Insurance savings on salary-sacrificed pension contributions above £2,000 a year from April 2029, so the rules may change — check GOV.UK for the latest position.

To see how a lower gross salary changes your tax and NI, try our [National Insurance and tax calculator](/national-insurance-and-tax-calculator-uk).

## Opting Out and Re-Enrolment

You have the right to opt out, but think carefully: you lose your employer's contribution and the tax relief, which is effectively a pay cut.

- **Within one month of enrolment:** You can opt out through the pension provider and get a full refund of what you have paid in.
- **After one month:** You can stop contributing at any time, but contributions already made normally stay invested until you can access them.
- **Your employer cannot encourage you to opt out** or offer you an incentive to do so. That is against the law.
- **Re-enrolment:** Roughly every three years, your employer must re-enrol staff who have opted out if they still meet the eligibility criteria. You can opt out again if you wish.

If money is tight, it may be better to keep contributing at the minimum rather than stopping entirely, so you continue to receive the employer contribution.

## Changing Jobs

When you leave an employer, your pension does not disappear. Contributions stop, but the pot stays invested in your name. Your new employer will auto-enrol you into its own scheme if you qualify, often after a short postponement period of up to three months.

When you move jobs:

1. Make a note of your old scheme's name, provider and your member number.
2. Give the provider your new address and personal email.
3. Check whether your new employer offers better contributions or matching.
4. Decide later whether to leave old pots where they are or combine them.

## Changing Your Name

If you change your name by deed poll, marriage or civil partnership, tell every pension provider you belong to, as well as your current employer's payroll. Mismatched names are a common reason pension payments and transfers get delayed. Providers will usually ask for a copy of your deed poll or certificate. Our guide on [who to notify after changing your name](/name-change/who-to-notify-after-changing-your-name-uk) includes pensions in its checklist.

## Finding Lost Pensions

With people changing jobs frequently, it is easy to lose track of small pots. To find them:

- **Search your paperwork** for annual statements, P60s or old payslips showing pension deductions.
- **Contact former employers** and ask which provider ran their scheme.
- **Use the Pension Tracing Service**, a free government service on GOV.UK that can find contact details for workplace and personal pension schemes. It does not tell you whether you have a pension or how much it is worth — you then contact the scheme directly.

Be wary of companies that charge a fee to find pensions, or that cold-call offering free pension reviews. Pension scams are common, and the Pension Tracing Service is free.

## Combining Pension Pots

Bringing several defined contribution pots together can make them easier to manage and may reduce charges. Before transferring, check whether an old scheme has:

- exit fees
- guaranteed annuity rates or other valuable guarantees
- a protected pension age that lets you take benefits earlier
- defined benefit rights, which usually require regulated financial advice before transferring if the value is above a set limit

MoneyHelper has free guides on transferring, and anyone aged 50 or over with a defined contribution pension can book a free Pension Wise appointment.

## State Pension Basics

Your workplace pension sits on top of the State Pension, which is based on your National Insurance record rather than on any particular job.

| Qualifying NI years | New State Pension entitlement |
| :--- | :--- |
| Fewer than 10 | Usually none |
| 10 to 34 | A proportion of the full amount |
| 35 or more | The full new State Pension |

You build qualifying years by paying NI through work or receiving NI credits — for example while claiming certain benefits or Child Benefit for a young child. You can check your State Pension forecast and NI record on GOV.UK, and see whether paying voluntary contributions could fill any gaps. State Pension age is set by law and can change, so check your own date on GOV.UK.

## Summary

If you are aged 22 to State Pension age and earn over £10,000 from a job, your employer must enrol you into a workplace pension and pay at least 3% of your qualifying earnings, with a minimum total of 8%. On a £30,000 salary, that is £1,900.80 a year going into your pension for a real cost to a basic rate taxpayer of £950.40. Find out whether your scheme uses net pay or relief at source — especially if you earn under £12,570 — and whether salary sacrifice is available. Think hard before opting out, keep a record of every scheme you join, update providers when you move or change your name, and use the free Pension Tracing Service to find old pots. Finally, check your State Pension forecast so you know where you stand on your 35 qualifying years.
