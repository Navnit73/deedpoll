# UK Tax Codes Explained

Your tax code is a short string of numbers and letters that appears on every payslip and pension statement, and most people never look at it twice. That's a mistake. The code is the instruction HMRC gives your employer or pension provider about how much tax to take, and if it's wrong, you'll be paying the wrong amount every single pay day until someone notices.

This guide explains what each part of a UK tax code means, how to work out your tax-free allowance from it, why codes change during the year, and what to do if yours looks wrong. All figures relate to the 2026/27 tax year, which runs from 6 April 2026 to 5 April 2027.

---

## What a Tax Code Actually Does

A tax code tells your employer how much of your pay to treat as tax-free before Income Tax is worked out. Everything above that tax-free amount is taxed at the rates that apply to you.

The code doesn't change how much tax you owe for the year — that's set by the law and your actual income. What it controls is how much tax is *collected* through payroll. A correct code means the tax taken over the year should match what you owe. An incorrect code means you'll either overpay (and need a refund) or underpay (and get a bill or a reduced code later).

---

## How to Read a Tax Code

Most codes have two parts: a **number** and a **letter**. Some also have a **prefix** (S or C) or a **suffix** (W1, M1 or X).

### The Number

Multiply the number by 10 and you get roughly the amount you can earn in the tax year before paying Income Tax on that job or pension.

- 1257L → £12,570 tax-free
- 1100L → £11,000 tax-free
- 957L → £9,570 tax-free

If the number is lower than 1257, HMRC has reduced your allowance — usually to collect tax on something else, such as a company car or a previous underpayment.

### The Letter

The letter tells your employer about your situation and, in some cases, how to apply the code.

### The Prefix

- **S** at the start means you're a Scottish taxpayer. Scotland has its own Income Tax bands and rates (starter, basic, intermediate, higher, advanced and top), which differ from the rest of the UK. Check GOV.UK for the current Scottish rates.
- **C** at the start means you're a Welsh taxpayer. Welsh rates are currently the same as in England and Northern Ireland.
- **No prefix** means you pay tax at the England and Northern Ireland rates.

## Common UK Tax Codes and Letters

| Code or letter | What it means |
| :--- | :--- |
| **1257L** | Standard tax-free Personal Allowance of £12,570 |
| **L** | You're entitled to the standard tax-free Personal Allowance |
| **M** | You're receiving Marriage Allowance — 10% of your partner's allowance (e.g. 1383M) |
| **N** | You've transferred 10% of your allowance to your partner under Marriage Allowance (e.g. 1131N) |
| **T** | Your code includes other calculations HMRC needs to review, such as a reduced allowance on income over £100,000 |
| **BR** | All income from this job or pension is taxed at the basic rate (20%), with no tax-free allowance — common for a second job |
| **D0** | All income from this source is taxed at the higher rate (40%) |
| **D1** | All income from this source is taxed at the additional rate (45%) |
| **NT** | No tax is taken from this income |
| **0T** | You have no tax-free allowance left, or HMRC doesn't have enough information to give you one |
| **K** | Your deductions exceed your allowances, so an amount is *added* to your taxable pay |
| **W1 / M1 / X** | Emergency code: tax is worked out on each pay period in isolation, not cumulatively |

Scottish taxpayers may see variations on the D codes (for example, codes beginning SD) reflecting the different Scottish bands.

## Worked Example: How Much Tax-Free Pay Does 1257L Give You?

Let's take someone on the standard code 1257L earning £30,000 a year, paid monthly, living in England.

**Step 1 — annual tax-free amount:** 1257 × 10 = £12,570

**Step 2 — monthly tax-free pay:** £12,570 ÷ 12 = approximately **£1,047.50 a month**

**Step 3 — monthly gross pay:** £30,000 ÷ 12 = £2,500

**Step 4 — taxable pay each month:** £2,500 − £1,047.50 = £1,452.50

**Step 5 — tax at 20%:** £1,452.50 × 20% = **£290.50 a month**

Over a full year that's £3,486 in Income Tax, which matches the annual calculation: (£30,000 − £12,570) × 20% = £3,486. Payroll software uses HMRC's tables, so the figure on your actual payslip may differ by a few pence or pounds from this rounded calculation.

On a weekly payroll, the tax-free amount is roughly £12,570 ÷ 52, or about £241.73 a week.

National Insurance is worked out separately and isn't affected by your tax code. To see your full take-home pay, including NI and pension contributions, try our [after-tax pay calculator](/after-tax-pay-calculator-uk).

## Emergency Tax Codes

An emergency code has **W1**, **M1** or **X** at the end — for example, 1257L W1 or 1257L M1.

Normally, PAYE works **cumulatively**: each pay day, your employer looks at your total pay and total tax so far in the tax year. If you've overpaid earlier in the year, the system automatically gives it back. Emergency codes switch this off. Each week or month is treated in isolation, as if it were the only pay period in the year.

### Why You Might Be on One

- You started a new job and didn't hand over a P45
- You started receiving a pension or a taxable state benefit
- You returned to work after a gap and HMRC's records weren't up to date
- You started getting company benefits, and HMRC hadn't yet worked out the full-year figures

### What to Do

Emergency codes are temporary. Once HMRC has the right information — usually after your employer submits your starter details — it sends a corrected code, and any overpayment is normally refunded through your pay. If you're still on an emergency code after a couple of pay periods, check your personal tax account and make sure your employment details are correct. If the tax year ends before it's fixed, you may need to claim a refund; our guide on [how to contact HMRC and claim a tax refund](/tax-money/how-to-contact-hmrc-and-claim-a-tax-refund) walks through the process.

## K Codes Explained

A K code is the opposite of a normal code. Instead of giving you tax-free pay, it **adds** an amount to your taxable income.

This happens when what HMRC needs to collect — for example, the taxable value of a company car and private medical insurance, or tax owed from a previous year — is larger than your Personal Allowance.

**Example:** You have the full £12,570 allowance, but benefits in kind worth £17,000. £17,000 − £12,570 = £4,430 more in deductions than allowances. Your code might be K443, meaning about £4,430 is added to your taxable pay over the year.

There's an important safeguard: under a K code, the tax deducted in any pay period can't be more than **50% of your pay** for that period. Any tax that can't be collected because of this limit will need to be dealt with separately.

## Why Your Tax Code Might Change

Codes aren't fixed for life. HMRC can issue a new one at any time during the year. Common reasons include:

### Benefits in Kind

If your employer gives you a company car, health insurance or other taxable perks, HMRC reduces your tax-free allowance by the taxable value. A £3,000 benefit would turn 1257L into **957L**.

### Collecting an Underpayment

If you paid too little tax in a previous year, HMRC will often collect it through your code rather than ask for a lump sum, usually where the debt is under set limits (commonly £3,000). The allowance reduction is grossed up to reflect your tax rate. For a basic-rate taxpayer owing £500, HMRC reduces the allowance by £2,500 (because £2,500 × 20% = £500), giving **1007L**.

### A Second Job or Pension

You only get one Personal Allowance. Usually it's all given to your main job, and the second job is taxed on **BR**. If your main job pays less than £12,570, you can ask HMRC to split your allowance between the two so you aren't overtaxed on the second.

### Income Over £100,000

Your Personal Allowance reduces by £1 for every £2 of adjusted net income over £100,000, and disappears completely at £125,140. HMRC may estimate this and issue a reduced code, often with a T suffix, or 0T.

### Untaxed Income

If you have smaller amounts of untaxed income — for example, rental profit or savings interest above your allowances — HMRC may collect tax on it through your code instead of requiring a Self Assessment return.

### Marriage Allowance

Claiming or ending Marriage Allowance changes both partners' codes to M or N.

## How to Check Your Tax Code

You'll find your code on:

- Your payslip
- Your P45 or P60
- A tax code notice (P2) from HMRC
- Your personal tax account or the HMRC app

The personal tax account is the most useful place, because it shows not just the code but *how it was worked out* — the allowances and deductions HMRC has used. Read this breakdown carefully. Check that the estimated income for each job is roughly right, that benefits listed are ones you actually receive, and that any underpayment being collected makes sense. Our guide on [how to check your Income Tax](/tax-money/how-to-check-your-income-tax-uk) covers this in more detail.

## How to Correct a Wrong Tax Code

1. **Find the cause.** Compare the breakdown in your personal tax account with your real situation.
2. **Update the details.** You can update estimated income, report a change in benefits, or tell HMRC about a job that has ended, through the personal tax account or the HMRC app.
3. **Call if needed.** If you can't fix it online, call the Income Tax helpline on 0300 200 3300 with your National Insurance number and a recent payslip.
4. **Wait for the new code.** HMRC sends it to your employer, which usually applies it from the next payroll run. Any overpayment is normally returned through your pay.
5. **Check it was applied.** Look at your next payslip to make sure the new code is being used.

Don't ask your employer to change the code themselves — they must use the one HMRC issues. If your circumstances are complicated, for example several income sources or large benefits in kind, it may be worth speaking to an accountant or HMRC directly.

## Summary

Your tax code is the bridge between what you owe and what's taken from your pay. The number, multiplied by 10, shows your tax-free amount; the letter explains your circumstances; an S or C prefix shows whether Scottish or Welsh rates apply; and a W1, M1 or X suffix means you're on a temporary emergency basis. The standard 1257L gives about £1,047.50 of tax-free pay a month. Codes change when you get benefits, start a second job, owe tax from an earlier year, or earn over £100,000. Check yours at the start of every tax year and whenever your circumstances change, and correct it through your personal tax account as soon as something looks off.
