# How to Read Your Payslip: A Line-by-Line UK Guide

Most people glance at the net pay figure at the bottom of their payslip and file it away. That is understandable, but it is also how mistakes go unnoticed for months. A wrong tax code, a missed pension contribution, or student loan deductions that should have stopped can quietly cost you hundreds of pounds.

This guide walks through a typical UK payslip from top to bottom, explains what each figure means and how it is calculated, and finishes with a full worked example for someone earning £30,000 a year in England in the 2026/27 tax year. It is general information, not personal financial advice — if your situation is complicated, speak to your payroll team, HMRC or a qualified adviser.

---

## Your Legal Right to a Payslip

Under the Employment Rights Act 1996, employees and workers are entitled to an itemised pay statement on or before the day they are paid. It can be on paper or electronic. As a minimum, it must show:

- your gross pay (before deductions)
- your net pay (what you actually receive)
- the amount of each deduction and what it is for, whether fixed or variable
- how net pay is split, if it is paid in different ways (for example, part by bank transfer and part in cash)
- the number of hours you are paid for, if your pay varies with the hours you work

If your employer does not give you a payslip, or the payslip leaves out required information, raise it with them in writing first. ACAS offers free, impartial guidance if the problem is not resolved.

## The Top Section: Who, When and Which Period

### Employer and Employee Details

The header normally shows your employer's name, your name, and an employee or payroll number. Check your name is spelled correctly and matches your HMRC records — this matters especially if you have recently changed your name by deed poll or marriage. If you have, our guide on [who to notify after changing your name](/name-change/who-to-notify-after-changing-your-name-uk) explains how to update your employer and HMRC.

### National Insurance Number

Your NI number (two letters, six digits, one letter, such as QQ 12 34 56 C) is how HMRC links your earnings and contributions to your record. An incorrect or missing NI number can mean your contributions are not credited properly, which can eventually affect your State Pension. If it is missing or wrong, tell payroll straight away.

### Tax Code

For 2026/27 the most common code is **1257L**, which reflects the standard Personal Allowance of £12,570. Letters and numbers can change if you have benefits in kind, more than one job, or an underpayment being collected. Watch for codes ending **W1**, **M1** or **X** — these are "non-cumulative" or emergency codes, which means each pay period is taxed in isolation and any overpayment will not be refunded automatically through payroll. Our guide to [UK tax codes explained](/tax-money/uk-tax-codes-explained) covers what each letter means.

### Tax Period, Tax Week or Tax Month

The UK tax year runs from 6 April to 5 April. Payroll numbers each pay period from that start date: monthly pay runs are numbered Month 1 to Month 12, weekly pay runs Week 1 to Week 52 (sometimes 53). Month 1 covers pay dates from 6 April to 5 May, Month 6 covers 6 September to 5 October, and so on. The period number matters because cumulative tax codes use it to work out how much tax-free allowance you have built up so far.

### Payment Date and Pay Method

You will usually see the date you are paid and sometimes the last four digits of the bank account the money is going to. If you have changed banks, check this.

## The Payments Section: How Your Gross Pay Is Built

### Basic Pay

Your contractual salary for the period. For an annual salary paid monthly, this is normally the salary divided by 12. For hourly workers, it should show the hours and the hourly rate — check the rate is at least the National Minimum Wage or National Living Wage for your age (current rates are on GOV.UK).

### Overtime and Additional Hours

Overtime is usually shown on a separate line with the hours and rate. If your contract promises enhanced rates (for example time-and-a-half), check the multiplier is applied. Overtime is often paid a month in arrears, so it may appear on the payslip after the month you worked it.

### Holiday Pay

If you have taken paid leave, it may be shown separately or simply included in basic pay. If you regularly work overtime or earn commission, holiday pay may need to reflect those regular earnings rather than basic pay alone — ask payroll how yours is calculated if it looks low.

### Statutory Payments: SSP, SMP and Others

If you have been off sick, you may see **Statutory Sick Pay (SSP)**. If you are on family leave, you might see **Statutory Maternity Pay (SMP)**, Statutory Paternity Pay, Shared Parental Pay or Statutory Adoption Pay. These are taxable and subject to National Insurance like normal pay. Your employer may also pay contractual sick or maternity pay on top. The rules and weekly rates for statutory payments change periodically, so check GOV.UK for the current figures if you want to verify the amount.

### Bonuses, Commission and Allowances

These appear as separate lines and are added to gross pay. A large one-off bonus can push a month's pay into the higher rate band, which is why tax on a bonus month often looks unexpectedly high. If your annual income stays below the higher rate threshold, cumulative tax codes generally even this out over the rest of the year.

## The Deductions Section

### PAYE Income Tax

Pay As You Earn tax is calculated on your taxable pay after subtracting your tax-free allowance for the period. In England, Wales and Northern Ireland for 2026/27, income above the £12,570 Personal Allowance is taxed at 20% up to £50,270, 40% up to £125,140 and 45% above that. Scotland has its own income tax bands and rates, so Scottish taxpayers (whose code starts with an **S**) will see different figures.

### National Insurance

Employees pay Class 1 National Insurance at 8% on earnings between £12,570 and £50,270 a year (or the weekly or monthly equivalent), and 2% on earnings above that. Unlike income tax, NI is worked out on each pay period on its own, not cumulatively, so a big bonus month can mean more NI even if your annual pay is modest. Your payslip may show an NI category letter — **A** is the standard letter for most employees.

### Pension Contributions

How your pension appears depends on how tax relief is given:

| Method | Where it appears | Effect on tax | Effect on NI |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Net pay arrangement | Deducted before tax is calculated | Reduces taxable pay | No effect |
| Relief at source | Deducted after tax | None on payslip; provider adds 20% relief to your pot | No effect |
| Salary sacrifice | Gross pay is reduced; employer pays it in | Reduces taxable pay | Reduces NI |

Under salary sacrifice you may see your basic salary reduced, or a negative line such as "Pension SS". The employer contribution normally does not show on your payslip at all, though some employers list it for information.

### Student Loan and Postgraduate Loan

If you have a student loan, you will see deductions for your plan type: Plan 1, Plan 2, Plan 4 (Scotland), Plan 5, and/or a Postgraduate Loan. You repay 9% of earnings above your plan's threshold (6% for Postgraduate Loans). Thresholds differ by plan and change over time, so check the current figures on GOV.UK. Like NI, student loan deductions are worked out per pay period. Make sure the plan type on your payslip matches your loan — being on the wrong plan is a surprisingly common error.

### Other Deductions

You may also see trade union subscriptions, give-as-you-earn charity donations, cycle-to-work or other salary sacrifice schemes, season ticket loan repayments, or attachment of earnings orders. Each should be labelled clearly. If you do not recognise a deduction, ask.

## Year-to-Date Figures

Most payslips have a box showing cumulative totals since 6 April: taxable pay to date, tax paid to date, NI to date, and pension to date. These are useful for two reasons:

1. **Checking your tax is on track.** If tax to date looks too high or too low relative to your pay to date, something may be wrong with your code.
2. **Matching your P60.** At the end of the year, your final payslip's year-to-date figures should match your P60.

## Worked Example: £30,000 Salary, Paid Monthly, England

Assumptions: 2026/27 tax year, tax code 1257L (cumulative), NI category A, no student loan, workplace pension at the auto-enrolment minimum of 5% of qualifying earnings using **relief at source**, so the pension deduction does not affect tax.

**Gross monthly pay:** £30,000 ÷ 12 = **£2,500.00**

**Income tax:**
- Monthly tax-free allowance: £12,570 ÷ 12 = £1,047.50
- Taxable pay: £2,500.00 − £1,047.50 = £1,452.50
- Tax at 20%: £1,452.50 × 0.20 = **£290.50**

**National Insurance:**
- Earnings above the monthly primary threshold: £2,500.00 − £1,047.50 = £1,452.50
- NI at 8%: £1,452.50 × 0.08 = **£116.20**

**Pension (relief at source):**
- Qualifying earnings: £30,000 − £6,240 = £23,760 a year, or £1,980 a month
- 5% gross contribution: £99.00 a month
- You pay 80% of that from net pay: **£79.20**; the provider claims the remaining £19.80 from HMRC
- Your employer adds at least 3% of qualifying earnings: £59.40 a month (not deducted from you)

| Payslip line | This month | Year to date (Month 6) |
| :--- | ---: | ---: |
| Basic pay | £2,500.00 | £15,000.00 |
| **Gross pay** | **£2,500.00** | **£15,000.00** |
| PAYE tax (1257L) | −£290.50 | −£1,743.00 |
| National Insurance (A) | −£116.20 | −£697.20 |
| Pension (relief at source) | −£79.20 | −£475.20 |
| **Net pay** | **£2,014.10** | **£12,084.60** |

Real payroll software uses HMRC's official tables, which round allowances and taxable pay in specific ways, so your actual figures may differ by a few pence or a pound — that is normal. Over a full year this works out at £3,486 tax and £1,394.40 NI. You can check your own figures with our [after tax pay calculator](/after-tax-pay-calculator-uk) or the [National Insurance and tax calculator](/national-insurance-and-tax-calculator-uk).

## How to Spot Mistakes: A Monthly Checklist

- **Tax code:** Is it the one you expect? Has it changed without explanation? Is there an emergency code (W1, M1, X, 0T or BR) still in use months after starting a job?
- **Gross pay:** Does it match your contract, hours worked and any overtime or bonus due?
- **NI number and category letter:** Present and correct?
- **Pension:** Is a contribution being deducted if you are enrolled, and at the rate you agreed?
- **Student loan:** Is the plan type right, and have deductions stopped if you have repaid in full?
- **Year-to-date totals:** Do they add up from previous payslips?
- **Your name and details:** Up to date and matching HMRC's records?

If tax looks wrong, check your personal tax account and see our guide on [how to check your Income Tax](/tax-money/how-to-check-your-income-tax-uk). Your employer must use the code HMRC sends, so code problems must be fixed with HMRC.

## P60, P45 and P11D

- **P60:** A summary of your pay and tax for the whole tax year from each employer you work for on 5 April. Employers must give it to you by 31 May. Keep it — you will need it to prove income or claim a refund.
- **P45:** Given when you leave a job. It shows your pay and tax to date and your tax code. Give parts of it to your new employer so they can tax you correctly and avoid an emergency code.
- **P11D:** Lists taxable benefits in kind, such as a company car or private medical insurance, if these are not taxed through payroll. Employers must provide it by 6 July after the end of the tax year.

## Summary

A payslip has three parts: who and when (your details, tax code and tax period), what you earned (basic pay plus overtime, holiday, statutory and bonus payments), and what was taken off (tax, National Insurance, pension, student loan and any other deductions). Checking your tax code, pension method, student loan plan and year-to-date totals each month takes a few minutes and is the easiest way to catch errors early. On a £30,000 salary in England in 2026/27, expect roughly £290.50 tax and £116.20 NI each month before pension. If something does not look right, ask payroll first, and go to HMRC for anything involving your tax code.
